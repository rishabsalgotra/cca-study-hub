Quantum Computing · Topic 1

Quantum computing basics, from qubits to algorithms

Quantum computers process information using the rules of quantum physics: superposition, interference and entanglement. This page builds every basic idea from scratch, with no physics degree required. Each concept comes with a picture, an interactive demo you can play with, and the minimal maths that makes it precise.

1. Why quantum computing?

Classical computers, from your phone to the largest supercomputer, store information as bits that are either 0 or 1, and change them with logic gates. They're extraordinarily good at almost everything. But some problems grow so fast with their size that no classical computer will ever solve them exactly. Simulating molecules is one example: describing 50 interacting electrons exactly needs more numbers than there are atoms in a planet.

A quantum computer stores information in qubits and manipulates it with quantum operations. For certain problems this lets it use an approach no classical machine can efficiently imitate: preparing a large superposition, then using interference so that wrong answers cancel out and right answers reinforce each other.

⚗️ Simulating nature

Chemistry, materials and drug discovery: molecules are quantum systems, so a quantum computer is a natural simulator. This is widely seen as the most likely first big win.

🔐 Cryptography

Shor's algorithm would break RSA and elliptic-curve cryptography on a large, error-corrected machine. That is why "post-quantum" cryptography is being rolled out now.

🔎 Search & optimisation

Grover's algorithm gives a quadratic speed-up for unstructured search. Heuristics for optimisation are being researched, but proven advantages there are limited.

🚫 Not a faster everything

Quantum computers won't speed up spreadsheets, web servers or video games. They are specialised accelerators for specific problem structures.

The one-sentence versionA quantum computer works with amplitudes, numbers that behave like waves, over many possible answers at once. It is useful only when an algorithm can make the amplitudes of wrong answers cancel and the right answer's amplitude grow before you look.

3. Superposition and amplitudes

A qubit in superposition isn't "secretly 0 or 1 and we just don't know which". It is in a genuinely different state, described by two amplitudes α and β. Amplitudes are not probabilities: they can be negative or complex. Probabilities only appear when you measure:

The Born ruleMeasuring α|0⟩ + β|1⟩ gives 0 with probability |α|² and 1 with probability |β|². Because something must happen, |α|² + |β|² = 1 (the state is normalised).

4. The Bloch sphere: picturing one qubit

Any single-qubit state can be written using two angles:

|ψ⟩ = cos(θ/2)|0⟩ + e^{iφ} sin(θ/2)|1⟩        θ ∈ [0°, 180°],  φ ∈ [0°, 360°)

Those two angles are the latitude and longitude of a point on a unit sphere, the Bloch sphere. The north pole is |0⟩ and the south pole is |1⟩. Points on the equator are equal superpositions that differ only in their relative phase φ. Every single-qubit gate is a rotation of this sphere.

⚠️ Common confusionOn the Bloch sphere, opposite points are orthogonal states: |0⟩ and |1⟩ are 180° apart on the sphere, even though their vectors are at 90° in the underlying space. That's why θ/2 appears in the formula. Also, a global phase (multiplying the whole state by e^{iγ}) has no physical effect and doesn't show up on the sphere.

5. Measurement

  • Measurement is irreversible. It returns a classical bit and leaves the qubit in the matching basis state. The amplitudes are destroyed.
  • You can't read amplitudes directly. To estimate them you prepare and measure the same circuit many times. Each run is a shot, and the counts estimate the probabilities.
  • Measurement has a basis. "Measuring in the X basis" means rotating with H first and then measuring 0/1. A |+⟩ state gives random results in the Z (0/1) basis but always gives "+" in the X basis.
  • Precision costs shots. Statistical error shrinks like 1/√shots, so a 10× more precise estimate needs about 100× more runs.
Why this matters for algorithmsSince one run returns only n classical bits from n qubits, a useful quantum algorithm must end in a state where the answer has high probability. Simply "computing all answers in parallel" and reading one at random is useless. Interference (next section) is what makes the right answer likely.

6. Phase and interference: the real source of power

Amplitudes add like waves. Two contributions with the same phase add up (constructive interference); with opposite phases they cancel (destructive interference). Quantum algorithms are choreographed so that paths leading to wrong answers cancel.

P(0) = cos²(φ/2). At φ = 0 the |1⟩ contributions cancel; at φ = 180° (a Z gate) the |0⟩ contributions cancel instead, so H·Z·H = X.

7. Quantum gates

A quantum gate is a unitary matrix U (one with U†U = I) applied to the state vector. Unitary means it preserves total probability and is reversible: every gate can be undone. That is very different from a classical AND gate, which throws information away.

The CNOT matrix is written with the control as the first (left) qubit and basis order |00⟩, |01⟩, |10⟩, |11⟩. Qiskit lists bits in the opposite order (qubit 0 rightmost), which swaps how this matrix looks.

  • Universality: {H, S, T, CNOT} (the "Clifford + T" set) can approximate any quantum computation to any desired accuracy, just as NAND gates suffice classically.
  • Clifford circuits are easy: circuits made only of H, S and CNOT can be simulated efficiently on a classical computer (the Gottesman–Knill theorem). The "magic" that makes quantum computing hard to simulate comes from non-Clifford gates like T.
  • Useful identities: H·H = I, X·X = I, HZH = X, HXH = Z, S² = Z, T² = S, T⁸ = I.

8. Many qubits: the exponential state space

Two qubits have four basis states, |00⟩, |01⟩, |10⟩, |11⟩, and a general two-qubit state needs four amplitudes. Every added qubit doubles the number of amplitudes: n qubits are described by 2ⁿ complex numbers. Combining independent qubits uses the tensor product, e.g. |0⟩ ⊗ |1⟩ = |01⟩.

QubitsAmplitudes (2ⁿ)Memory to store the state classically (16 bytes each)
101,02416 KiB: trivial
30≈ 1.07 billion16 GiB: a laptop
40≈ 1.1 trillion16 TiB: a large cluster
50≈ 1.1 × 10¹⁵16 PiB: beyond most supercomputers
300≈ 2 × 10⁹⁰More numbers than atoms in the observable universe (~10⁸⁰)
Exponential state ≠ exponential output2ⁿ amplitudes don't mean 2ⁿ answers you can read. Measurement returns just n bits. The exponential space is only useful when an algorithm uses structure (interference, periodicity) to concentrate probability on the answer. Also, many specific circuits can be simulated far more cheaply than the worst case (tensor networks, Clifford simulation), which is why "quantum advantage" claims are carefully contested.

9. Entanglement

Some multi-qubit states can't be written as a product of single-qubit states. These are entangled. The classic example is the Bell state, made with just two gates:

|00⟩  —H on q0→  (|0⟩+|1⟩)/√2 ⊗ |0⟩  —CNOT(q0→q1)→  (|00⟩ + |11⟩)/√2   ← a Bell state
Bell stateMeasurement correlationMade from
|Φ⁺⟩ = (|00⟩ + |11⟩)/√2Always equalH, CNOT
|Φ⁻⟩ = (|00⟩ − |11⟩)/√2Always equal (differs in phase)H, CNOT, then Z
|Ψ⁺⟩ = (|01⟩ + |10⟩)/√2Always oppositeH, CNOT, then X
|Ψ⁻⟩ = (|01⟩ − |10⟩)/√2Always opposite (in every basis)H, CNOT, then X and Z
⚠️ Entanglement doesn't send messagesMeasuring one qubit of a Bell pair instantly tells you the other's result, even light-years away, but you can't choose the outcome, so no information travels faster than light (the no-signalling theorem). Entanglement is a resource: it powers teleportation, superdense coding, quantum key distribution and the speed-ups of quantum algorithms. Bell-test experiments show its correlations can't be explained by hidden pre-agreed values.

11. The rules of the game

📵 No-cloning

There's no operation that copies an unknown quantum state. You can't back up a qubit or fan it out like a classical wire. This is why quantum error correction is so different, and why quantum key distribution can detect eavesdroppers.

↩️ Reversibility

Every gate is unitary and can be undone. Only measurement (and noise) is irreversible. Classical logic inside quantum algorithms must also be made reversible, often using extra "ancilla" qubits.

👁️ Observation disturbs

Measuring, or any uncontrolled interaction with the environment, collapses superpositions. Qubits must be extremely well isolated, yet still controllable.

📡 Teleportation

With one shared Bell pair plus two classical bits, a qubit's state can be moved to a distant qubit. The original is destroyed (consistent with no-cloning), and nothing travels faster than light.

12. Noise, decoherence and error correction

Real qubits are fragile. Tiny interactions with the environment, such as heat, stray fields or vibrations, gradually destroy the quantum information. This is decoherence.

Quantum error correction

You can't copy qubits, but you can spread one logical qubit across many physical qubits so that errors can be detected without measuring the encoded data. Helper qubits measure parity checks (syndromes), which reveal where an error happened but not the logical value.

EraWhat it means
NISQ (Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum)Tens to thousands of physical qubits without full error correction. Circuits must be short; results need error mitigation and many shots.
Early fault-tolerantFirst logical qubits whose error rates improve as the code grows (experiments since 2023–2024 have shown this "below threshold" behaviour on small codes).
Fault-tolerant (FTQC)Many logical qubits running deep circuits reliably. This is needed for Shor-scale cryptanalysis and most proven speed-ups, and likely requires hundreds to thousands of physical qubits per logical qubit.

Grover's algorithm in one picture: start with equal amplitudes on all N items; the oracle flips the sign of the marked item's amplitude; the diffusion step reflects every amplitude about the mean, which boosts the marked one. After ~(π/4)√N rounds, the marked item dominates. With N = 4 a single round is exact, as the "Grover finds |11⟩" preset in the circuit lab shows.

14. Quantum computing and cryptography

CryptographyQuantum threatResponse
RSA, Diffie–Hellman, elliptic-curve (ECDH, ECDSA)Broken by Shor's algorithm on a large fault-tolerant machineMigrate to post-quantum algorithms
Symmetric ciphers (AES)Grover roughly halves the effective key lengthUse 256-bit keys (AES-256)
Hash functions (SHA-2, SHA-3)Modest weakeningUse adequate output sizes
  • NIST post-quantum standards (August 2024): FIPS 203 ML-KEM (key encapsulation, from CRYSTALS-Kyber), FIPS 204 ML-DSA (signatures, from CRYSTALS-Dilithium) and FIPS 205 SLH-DSA (hash-based signatures, from SPHINCS+). They run on ordinary classical computers.
  • "Harvest now, decrypt later": adversaries can record encrypted traffic today and decrypt it once a capable quantum computer exists. Data that must stay secret for many years is already at risk, which is why migration is urgent even though no such machine exists yet.
  • Quantum key distribution (QKD) uses quantum physics itself (no-cloning) to detect eavesdropping on key exchange. It is a different, hardware-based approach from post-quantum algorithms.

DiVincenzo's criteria: what any quantum computer needs

  1. Scalable, well-characterised qubitsYou know exactly what your two-level systems are, and you can add more.
  2. InitialisationReliably reset qubits to a known state such as |0⟩.
  3. Long coherence relative to gate timeMany operations must fit within T₁ and T₂.
  4. A universal set of gatesSingle-qubit rotations plus at least one entangling gate.
  5. Qubit-specific measurementRead out individual qubits accurately.

For quantum networks, two more criteria cover converting between stationary and "flying" qubits (usually photons) and transmitting them faithfully.

16. Your first quantum program (Qiskit)

Qiskit is an open-source Python SDK for building circuits and running them on simulators or real hardware. This program was run with Qiskit 2.5 (pip install qiskit):

from qiskit import QuantumCircuit
from qiskit.primitives import StatevectorSampler
from qiskit.quantum_info import Statevector

qc = QuantumCircuit(2)
qc.h(0)        # put qubit 0 into an equal superposition
qc.cx(0, 1)    # entangle: flip qubit 1 when qubit 0 is 1

print(Statevector.from_instruction(qc))     # amplitudes before measurement
print(qc.draw())

qc.measure_all()
result = StatevectorSampler().run([qc], shots=1000).result()
print(result[0].data.meas.get_counts())     # only '00' and '11', roughly 50/50

17. Myths vs reality

MythReality
"A quantum computer tries every answer in parallel and picks the best."It evolves amplitudes over all inputs, but measurement returns one random outcome. Only interference that favours the answer makes this useful.
"Quantum computers will replace classical computers."They'll be specialised co-processors working alongside classical systems, which also handle control, decoding and pre- and post-processing.
"More qubits means more power."Quality matters as much as quantity: error rates, coherence, connectivity and the number of logical qubits decide what you can run.
"Quantum computers can solve NP-complete problems efficiently."Not believed to be true. Grover gives only a quadratic speed-up for brute-force search.
"Entanglement allows faster-than-light communication."No. Outcomes are random, and correlations only appear when results are compared over a classical channel.
"Encryption is already broken."Not by any existing machine. But "harvest now, decrypt later" makes migrating to post-quantum cryptography urgent for long-lived secrets.

Glossary

AmplitudeComplex number attached to each basis state; |amplitude|² is its measurement probability.
Basis stateA classical-looking state such as |0⟩, |1⟩ or |101⟩.
Bloch sphereGeometric picture of a single-qubit state as a point on a sphere.
Circuit depthThe number of sequential gate layers; deeper circuits suffer more noise.
Coherence time (T₁, T₂)How long a qubit keeps its energy state (T₁) and its phase (T₂).
EntanglementCorrelation between qubits that can't be described qubit by qubit.
FidelityHow close an actual operation or state is to the ideal one (1 = perfect).
Logical qubitAn error-corrected qubit encoded across many physical qubits.
OracleA black-box subroutine that marks solutions (e.g. by flipping a phase).
ShotOne execution of a circuit ending in measurement.
SuperpositionA state that is a weighted combination of basis states.
UnitaryA reversible, probability-preserving operation, i.e. a quantum gate.

Self-check quiz

Score: 0 / 0 answered

1 · A qubit is in the state 0.6|0⟩ + 0.8|1⟩. What is the probability of measuring 1?
B. Probability = |amplitude|² = 0.8² = 0.64 (and 0.6² = 0.36 for 0; they sum to 1).
2 · |+⟩ and |−⟩ give 50/50 results when measured in the 0/1 basis. How can you tell them apart?
D. They differ in relative phase. Interference (H) converts that phase difference into a measurable probability difference.
3 · What does H applied twice to |0⟩ give?
A. H·H = I. This is destructive interference in action, just like the interferometer where D1 never clicks.
4 · After H on q0 then CNOT(q0→q1), which outcomes can you measure?
C. It's the Bell state (|00⟩ + |11⟩)/√2: random but perfectly correlated.
5 · Why can't you simply copy a qubit to protect it from errors?
B. Quantum error correction measures parity checks (syndromes) that reveal errors without revealing or copying the encoded data.
6 · How many complex amplitudes describe a general state of 20 qubits?
D. Each extra qubit doubles the state space. That's why exact classical simulation hits a wall around 45–50 qubits.
7 · Which cryptography does Shor's algorithm threaten?
A. Shor solves factoring and discrete logarithms efficiently. AES is only weakened by Grover (use 256-bit keys), and ML-KEM is designed to resist quantum attacks.
8 · Grover's algorithm searches N unsorted items in roughly how many queries?
C. A quadratic speed-up, and provably the best possible for unstructured search.
